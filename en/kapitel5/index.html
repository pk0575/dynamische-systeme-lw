  • 5.1From Coupled Cells to the Cable EquationNeighboring heart muscle cells are coupled by gap junctions. If the distance Δx between the cells tends to zero, the system of ordinary differential equations becomes the cable equation ∂v/∂t = D ∂2v/∂x2 + … , a partial differential equation. Numerically it is solved again on a grid with Euler's method; this requires Δt < Δx2/(2D). The programs for Sections 5.2 to 5.4 show how this is done.0 figs.
  • 5.2Traveling WavesOn a fiber the excitation travels as a pulse with fixed shape and speed. For the front, the speed c = √(D/(2τin)) (1 − 3vs*) can be given exactly; it grows with the square root of the coupling D. The programs use D = 0.1 mm2/ms, Δx = 0.1 mm, and Δt = 0.01 ms.2 figs. · Apps
  • 5.3Circulating ExcitationOn a ring the wave travels in one direction and, after one round trip, meets the tissue it excited itself. If the ring is shorter than about the wavelength, this tissue is still refractory and the excitation dies out.1 figs.
  • 5.4Spiral Waves in the PlaneIn a layer of tissue the second derivative is replaced by the Laplace operator Δv. A second stimulus in partially refractory tissue creates a spiral wave that excites the tissue again and again without further stimuli – a model of ventricular tachycardia.2 figs. · Apps
  • 5.5From Tissue to the Whole HeartIn the heart, excitation spreads in three dimensions, faster along the fibers, whose direction rotates through the heart wall. Current heart models combine detailed cell models, the bidomain model, and patient-specific geometries; the simulation in Figure 5.7 of the book was computed with the freely available software openCARP on a laptop.0 figs.
